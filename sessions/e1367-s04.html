Gentzkow and Shapiro: slant as the language of Congress

The question. What determines the political slant of news: readers, or owners, politicians and journalists? The stake is regulatory. US ownership rules assume that diverse owners produce diverse viewpoints.

The measure. From the 2005 Congressional Record, pick the 1,000 two- and three-word phrases that best separate Democratic from Republican speakers: "death tax", "tax relief", "war on terror" against "estate tax", "tax break", "war in Iraq". Then score each newspaper by how Republican a member of Congress using its phrase mix would be. Applied to the news content of 433 dailies, 74% of US daily circulation. Honest about its limits: among members of Congress the index correlates 0.61 with true ideology, so the authors estimate that about 63% of the variance in newspaper slant is noise.

The identification. Demand: within one market, does a right-leaning paper sell relatively more in Republican zip codes? Using only within-market variation sidesteps the fact that papers choose slant to suit their market. Supply: is slant higher where the market votes Republican, with church attendance as an instrument for the market's politics, and do co-owned papers share a slant once geography is accounted for?

  • Readers prefer like-minded news, robust to zip-code fixed effects.
  • Ten points more Bush vote share moves slant by 0.015. Reader ideology explains about 20% of the variance in slant (22% within states).
  • Owners explain about 4%, statistically indistinguishable from zero. The raw similarity of co-owned papers disappears once geography is controlled for: chains buy papers in similar places.
  • The average paper sits close to its profit-maximising slant (0.473 against 0.460). Moving one standard deviation away costs about 18% of variable profit, imprecisely estimated.

The weak point. The index cannot separate what a paper covers from how it covers it. A paper that simply reports more on estate taxes scores Republican even with neutral framing, and the phrase list is frozen to one year of one Congress. The authors say openly there is no benchmark of unbiased reporting. On the supply side, church attendance must affect slant only through readers' politics.

Read the conclusion's caveats. Owners may still steer coverage where their interest is strong; results may not travel to other institutions (they cite Berlusconi); a local monopoly need not deliver the right amount of diversity. Session 6, on ownership, starts from exactly these.

Ciccarone: slant in pictures, under limited attention

The question. Slant measures read text and assume a reader who takes all of it in. Most political news is now video, watched in fragments. How much partisan content travels through images, and does that change when attention is short?

The measure. Train on 15,427 US political TV ads (2016–2020), labelled by the sponsor's party. Embed frames and transcript chunks with CLIP, a model that places images and text in one shared space, and fit one classifier per modality. Images alone reach an out-of-sample AUC of 0.911, text 0.909, both 0.945. Then apply the classifiers unchanged to 5,156 YouTube news videos on immigration from Fox News, MSNBC, CNN and others. The logic is Gentzkow and Shapiro's: learn partisanship from politicians' own output, then look for it in the media.

  • Full attention: text is the stronger single signal, but on its own it understates the Fox–MSNBC gap by 5.5 points, about a fifth.
  • Short attention: scoring random 5-second chunks, images win. Twenty seconds of images carry as much partisan signal as 45 seconds (about 137 words) of text; one frame is worth about 40 words.
  • The experiment: about 3,400 Prolific respondents, half Republican, watch real Fox and MSNBC footage of one immigration event with visuals and narration recombined, in 30-second or 2-minute clips. Republican images raise perceived Republican leaning by 0.2 SD at both lengths, raise fear and anger, and in short clips reduce donations to a pro-immigrant charity, but do not move policy attitudes. Republican text moves attitudes by about 0.07 SD, and only in long clips.

The weak points. "Partisan" means resembling one party's ads, which are persuasion products with their own visual grammar; the author notes immigration content scores Republican as a topic. The attention result measures how fast a CLIP-based classifier accumulates signal, not how fast viewers do, and CLIP's text side was built for short captions rather than speech. In the experiment, "Republican images" means one broadcast's footage, and the narration is re-voiced with a generated voice, which strips out tone.

The discussion question worth having ready: both papers define slant as similarity to what partisans say. Is that bias, or coverage of the issues one party owns? And if images carry emotion rather than argument, does an index built for text still measure what matters for persuasion?

What you should be able to do afterwards

  • Explain the Gentzkow–Shapiro index in two sentences, and why a 0.61 correlation implies that most measured slant is noise
  • Say why within-market variation identifies demand even though slant is chosen to suit the market, and what the church-attendance instrument has to assume
  • State the 20% against 4% result, and the caveats the authors attach to it
  • Say what "an image is worth 40 words" describes (a classifier) and which part of the paper speaks to actual viewers
  • Propose one change to the experiment that would separate "images" from "this particular footage"
