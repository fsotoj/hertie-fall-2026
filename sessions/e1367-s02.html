The reading

Both required, both on Moodle. Fifty-three journal pages and thirty.

Snyder, J.M. Jr. and Strömberg, D. (2010), "Press Coverage and Political Accountability", Journal of Political Economy 118(2), pp. 355–408. [United States]

Banerjee, A., Enevoldsen, N.T., Pande, R. and Walton, M. (2024), "Public Information is an Incentive for Politicians: Experimental Evidence from Delhi Elections", American Economic Journal: Applied Economics 16(3), pp. 323–353. [India]

The summaries below come from the published abstracts. Start with Snyder and Strömberg: it is the longer of the two and it is the design template the rest of the course varies.

Snyder and Strömberg: coverage as an accident of geography

The problem. Press coverage of a politician is not randomly assigned. Places that care about politics get more of it, and caring about politics also moves everything else the outcome depends on.

The instrument. Congruence: how well a newspaper's market overlaps a congressional district. It is high when most people in the district read paper X and most of paper X's readers live in that district. Congruence is largely an artefact of where newspaper markets happened to be drawn relative to district lines, and the paper adds variation from redistricting, which moves boundaries for reasons unconnected to any individual representative.

The chain, which is why it opens the course. Low congruence reduces coverage of the House member. Voters in those areas are less likely to recall their representative's name and less able to describe or rate them. Representatives facing less coverage behave less like agents of the district: less witness testimony, less constituency-oriented committee service, more party-line voting. And federal spending flowing to the district falls.

That is the whole information-to-policy chain estimated in one paper, each link measured separately. Take the structure rather than the coefficients: a source of variation in media exposure that is plausibly unrelated to the outcome, and then every step from exposure to policy.

Banerjee, Enevoldsen, Pande and Walton: publicity as an incentive

The design. Two years before the elections, two-thirds of Delhi municipal councillors learned they had been randomly selected for a pre-election newspaper report card on their record. The treatment is not information reaching voters at the moment of voting. It is the councillor knowing, years in advance, that their record will be published.

  • Treated councillors in high-slum wards increased pro-poor spending, relative both to control councillors and to treated councillors in low-slum wards. That second comparison is the one doing the work: the incentive bites only where the audience of the report card is the constituency such spending serves.
  • Randomly assigned gender quotas make some incumbents ineligible to run again in their home ward. Those treated incumbents were substantially likelier to run elsewhere only if their report card showed a strong pro-poor record, which is career concerns showing up as a portable reputation.
  • Parties also benefited electorally from their councillors' pro-poor spending.
  • The cross-cut arm is the punch: councillors given actionable information that was not publicly disclosed did not react. So the treatment is publicity, not information.

Why the two are assigned together

Same question, opposite ends of the design space and of the causal chain.

Snyder & StrömbergBanerjee et al.
Observational, instrument from market geography and redistrictingField experiment, randomised at the councillor level
Varies how much coverage existsVaries whether a record will be published
Voters become informed, then politicians respondPoliticians anticipate, before voters see anything
Threat: is congruence really unrelated to district characteristicsThreat: is a one-off report card the same thing as a press ecosystem

The last row is the discussion question worth having ready. An experiment buys clean identification of a treatment no real media market delivers; an instrument buys a real media market at the price of an assumption. Ornaghi's stated aim for the seminar is methodology and policy, so that is the register the room is asking for.

What you should be able to do afterwards

  • State congruence in one sentence, and say what would break it as an instrument
  • Explain why the low-slum comparison group is what identifies the incentive channel in Delhi
  • Distinguish voters being informed from politicians expecting voters to be informed, and point to the arm of the experiment that separates them
  • Say which link of the Snyder and Strömberg chain you would attack first, and with what evidence

Prerequisites the syllabus assumes: Economics I for the framework, Statistics I to read the tables, Statistics II for the quasi-experimental designs. Congruence is used as an instrument, so the IV assumptions are the ones to have fresh.
