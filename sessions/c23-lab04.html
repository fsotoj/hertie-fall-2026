What is already known: it opens with the Poisson

The lab 3 handout says so in its first paragraph: "The Poisson, the fourth member of that tour, opens lab 4." What comes after it is not stated anywhere.

The story. The number of events when there are many opportunities and each is unlikely: emails in an hour, typos on a page, earthquakes in a year.

The PMF is P(X = k) = e−λλk/k! for k = 0, 1, 2, …, with no upper limit. It sums to one because Σ λk/k! = eλ, the Taylor series of the exponential from this week's calculus reading: a clean bridge between the two halves of the course.

Mean and variance are both λ, which is the quick check for whether count data look Poisson. And Bin(n, p) is close to Pois(np) when n is large and p small: the probability of zero successes is 0.1326 for Bin(100, 0.02) and 0.1353 for Pois(2).

It is section 4.7 of Blitzstein and Hwang, inside the session 3 reading range. The lecture 3 slides never showed it, so it only matters for quiz 2 if the lecture 4 deck brings it in.

The rest of the lab is probably this week's calculus done numerically, since each lab so far has followed the lecture of its week. That is an inference. The environment has not changed: NumPy, pandas, Matplotlib and SciPy, no symbolic algebra package.

What you should be able to do afterwards

  • Recognise a Poisson situation from the story, and write its PMF
  • Say why the Poisson PMF sums to one, using the series for eλ
  • Use the Poisson as an approximation to the Binomial, and say when it is good
  • Simulate Poisson counts and compare their mean and variance
