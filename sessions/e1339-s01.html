What is data science?

The reading

Required first, then the two optional books that the rest of the course leans on and that are free online.

Kelleher, J. and B. Tierney (2018), Data Science, MIT Press Essential Knowledge series, chapter 1, "What Is Data Science?" On Moodle.

Salganik, M. (2017), Bit by Bit: Social Research in the Digital Age, Princeton University Press, chapters 1 and 2. Free at bitbybitbook.com.

Optional: R4DS chapters 1–4 (r4ds.hadley.nz), the course's main textbook, and Advanced R chapters 1–5 (adv-r.hadley.nz), which the syllabus recommends only if you already have substantial R experience.

What the two readings are for

They answer the same question from opposite ends: what the field is made of, and what it does to research.

Kelleher and Tierney define the field by its lineage. The chapter's thread is the 1990s problem that produced it: organisations accumulating data they could not analyse, held in separate databases that were built for storage and retrieval rather than for analytical work, and the data warehouse as the answer to that. Statistics, machine learning, database engineering and what got called big data arrive as the components. Read it for the vocabulary and the boundaries of the term, not for method.

Salganik chapter 2, "Observing Behavior", is the one with structure to hold on to. It sets out ten common characteristics of big data, and the list is worth memorising because half of it is bad news: big, always-on and nonreactive on the good side; incomplete, inaccessible, nonrepresentative, drifting, algorithmically confounded, dirty and sensitive on the other. Then three research strategies that work with such data anyway: counting things, forecasting and nowcasting, and approximating experiments.

Chapter 1 is the shorter setup: what changes when research moves into the digital age, and the distinction the book runs on between data made for research and data made for something else that a researcher repurposes. Both chapters are about judgement rather than tooling, which is the point of putting them in week one.

Setup and rules that differ from the other courses

Software. RStudio/Posit, Markdown and Git/GitHub, with basic R assumed. Everything is submitted through GitHub, so the account and the workflow need to exist before the first homework is due rather than on the day.

The AI policy is the strict one. Generative AI is permitted only for specific assignments, against the instructions given for each one, and a Statement of Authorship goes in every written assignment. Economics of Crime permits it throughout the course; do not carry that habit across.

Attendance. Two of twelve, if no course assignments are affected, and Munzert states that he does not want to be told in advance.

What you should be able to do afterwards

  • Say what data science is made of, and which parts are older than the label
  • Recite the ten characteristics of big data, and name which ones are problems
  • Match a research question to counting, forecasting or approximating an experiment
  • Explain why nonrepresentative and algorithmically confounded are different failures
  • Have R, RStudio, Git and a GitHub account working before the first homework
Looking ahead

The course is four blocks. Fundamentals, sessions 1–4: this one, then functions and debugging, iteration and automation, and the command line. Collecting data, 5–7: web data and technologies, web scraping and APIs, relational databases and SQL. Analyzing data, 8–9: modeling and visualization, with the hackathon straight after. Recurring parts of the workflow, 10–12: monitoring and communication, ethics, and AI in the data science workflow.

Session 2, Thursday 10 September, is Programming I: functional programming tools and a general debugging strategy before the specific tools. R4DS chapter 25 and Advanced R chapter 22. The first homework is due that morning at 10:00.
