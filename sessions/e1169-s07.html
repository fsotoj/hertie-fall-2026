Fines and monetary sanctions: what they buy, what they cost

Wednesday 14 October 2026, 10:00–12:00, room 2.32. Christian Traxler. Seminar session, the last before the fall break. Two required papers that split the question in half: in Florida a $195 speeding fine pushes some households into default, and in Finland an income-based fine deters nobody in advance but cuts reoffending for a few months once it lands.

Mello: can households absorb a speeding ticket?

The cost side of a fine, measured in credit reports.

Design. A staggered event study on the timing of a speeding stop in Florida: drivers stopped this quarter are compared only with drivers who will be stopped later. Speeding stops only, because tickets for broken lights or expired papers can themselves signal money trouble, and they show it in the pre-trends while speeding does not. First stop in at least a year, so licence suspensions for accumulated points stay out.

Data. All Florida traffic citations 2011–2015 linked to monthly credit reports and to payroll records from a subset of large employers; 525,646 drivers. Fines range from $123 to $273, about $195 on average.

Result. Unpaid bills in collections rise by about $34, so roughly a sixth of the average fine is paid for by not paying something else. Drivers with spare room on a credit card borrow instead; those without default more, about $44. Three years later credit scores are 2.6 points lower and borrowing limits $330 lower, and the chance of appearing in the large-employer payroll records is 1.2 points lower, about 8%. The effect runs through paying the fine itself, not through licence suspensions or insurance. The author's reading: at least 11%, and plausibly 38%, of households cannot cover a surprise expense of about $300 without defaulting elsewhere.

Where to push. Everyone pays about the same fine, so the paper measures the effect of getting a ticket, not of its size. It shows that fines hurt fragile households, but cannot say what a smaller, income-scaled or instalment fine would do, which is the policy question of the session. Fallbacks: a stop bundles fine, time and insurance costs; car purchases rise just before stops; and "employment" here means employment at large, well-paying firms.

Kaila: do income-based fines deter?

Finnish day fines, a threshold at 20 km/h over the limit, and no cap.

Setting. Up to 20 km/h over the limit a Finnish driver pays a fixed fine; above it the fine becomes a number of day fines times a value set by net monthly income, with no upper limit. At the same speed the jump is about €50 for the bottom income quartile and about €500 for the top.

Design. Two steps. First, a bunching test: a Becker driver should slow down and pile up just below such a jump. Second, because nobody does, a regression discontinuity: drivers caught by a camera just above and just below the threshold are comparable but receive very different fines, so their later reoffending can be compared. Licence points and suspensions do not change at the threshold.

Result. No bunching, not even among top earners. But the fine received matters: crossing the threshold raises the fine by about €200 on average and cuts the chance of another traffic offence within six months by 2.2 points, about 15%. The effect fades and is no longer significant after a year. It is concentrated among high earners, who face the biggest jumps: −4.5 points in the top quartile, nothing in the bottom one. Kaila's explanation is a Becker model with a simplifying heuristic (drivers treat the schedule as smooth) and learning from the fine they get. A flat fine delivering the same deterrence would be about €215: quadruple the current jump for the bottom quartile, less than half of it for the top.

Where to push. The outcome is being caught again by a camera, not speeding. A driver who learns from a big fine may slow down, or may learn where the cameras are; the paper cannot separate the two. Fallbacks: the learning model fits the facts but is not tested against alternatives, and the effect is short-lived, so large fines deter only with frequent enforcement.

How the two fit together

  • Two halves of the cost-benefit of a fine. Kaila measures what a fine buys in deterrence, Mello what it costs the people fined. Neither measures both.
  • The equity argument for day fines. Kaila cites Mello for the point that flat fines are regressive. A flat fine with equal deterrence would take a much larger bite from exactly the low-income drivers Mello shows defaulting.
  • Variation in the fine. Mello has none, ticket against no ticket; Kaila has a roughly tenfold range at the same speed. Only the second can speak to how large a fine should be.
  • The instructor's own work sits in the middle. Kaila contrasts his no-bunching result with clear bunching below German speeding-fine notches (Traxler et al. 2018), and reads the difference as salience: a simple schedule is understood, a complex one is not. Traxler and Dušek add the revenue side: higher fines are still mostly paid.

What you should be able to do afterwards

  • Distinguish general from specific deterrence, and say why "no bunching" plus "a response to the fine received" points to information frictions
  • Explain what Kaila's discontinuity at 20 km/h identifies, and what has to be smooth at the threshold
  • Explain what Mello's event study compares, and why it uses speeding stops only
  • Make the equity case for income-based fines with the numbers above
  • Say what a fine can do that prison cannot, and what can go wrong with it
