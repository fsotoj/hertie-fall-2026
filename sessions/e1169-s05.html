Prisons: sentence length, social interaction and release

Wednesday 30 September 2026, 10:00–12:00, room 2.32. Christian Traxler. Seminar session: two required papers built on the same Italian natural experiment, the 2006 collective pardon. One asks whether a longer expected sentence deters the person who faces it; the other asks whether it also deters the people around them.

The experiment both papers use

One law, and one clause in it, turn a prison release into a randomised sanction.

The pardon. In July 2006, to relieve overcrowding, the Italian Parliament cut three years from the sentence of every inmate whose crime predated 2 May 2006 (mafia, terrorism, sex crimes and a few others excluded). About 22,000 inmates were released on 1 August 2006; the prison population fell from 60,710 to 38,847 overnight.

The clause. Anyone who reoffends within five years and receives a new sentence of more than two years must also serve the pardoned remainder, between 1 and 36 months, on top. Two inmates with the same original sentence, released the same day, face different add-ons only because one entered prison earlier. Taking the entry date as good as random, the add-on is a randomly assigned increase in the expected punishment for any future crime. Everyone is out, so incapacitation plays no role.

Drago, Galbiati and Vertova: does a longer expected sentence deter?

General deterrence, measured on people who have just left prison.

Data. Prison administration records on everyone released under the pardon, with re-arrest observed until the end of February 2007, about seven months. In the working paper, roughly 20,000 men, 12% re-arrested, an average add-on of about 15 months.

Result, from the published abstract. One month less served, turned into one month more of expected sentence, lowers the probability of re-arrest by 0.16 percentage points, an elasticity of recidivism with respect to expected punishment of −0.74 over seven months. On a 12% base that is roughly 1.3% per month. The working paper adds that the response fades for long sentences and weakens with time already served, which it reads as evidence against specific deterrence; whether that survives in the journal version was not checked.

Where to push. The treatment is a bundle: for a given sentence, one more month of add-on is also one month less in prison. Less time inside could itself lower reoffending. The authors answer with a placebo on short sentences, where the add-on rarely bites, and by controlling for time served within sentence bands. Whether that fully separates the two is the sharp question. Fallbacks: seven months of re-arrest measures detection over a short window, and pardoned ex-inmates are a group already not deterred once.

Drago and Galbiati: do sanctions spread through networks?

Same pardon; now the treatment is what happened to everyone else.

Design. The variable of interest is the average add-on of your peers, holding fixed your own sentence, your own add-on and your peers' original sentences. Peers are inmates released from the same prison and of the same nationality (for Italians, the same region). Because the treatment is peers' add-ons rather than peers' characteristics, who ends up in which group does not need to be random: this is the paper's way around Manski's reflection problem.

Results, from the working paper. Your own add-on: one more month lowers re-arrest by about 0.16 points. Your peers' average add-on: one more month lowers your re-arrest by about 0.20 points, at least as much as your own. Raising everyone's add-on by a month cuts group recidivism by about 2.8%, roughly half of it through peers, hence a social multiplier of about 2 (also in the published abstract). Randomly reshuffling nationality within a prison, or prison within a nationality, makes the effect vanish.

Where to push. The multiplier assumes peers' add-ons affect you only through peers' crime. Two other channels give the same indirect effect without any contagion: co-offenders who stop being available, and peers' sanctions making the law more salient to you. Which of these can the data rule out? Fallback: the peer groups are assumed, not observed.

How the two fit together

  • One shock, two effects. The direct response to your own expected sanction, and the indirect response to everyone else's. Together they are the full effect of the policy.
  • Threat versus experience. Both papers measure general deterrence, the effect of a threat. The optional Finnish paper measures the effect of the prison experience itself and finds it deters financial-crime defendants, while the Italian working paper found that more time served weakens deterrence. Different populations are the obvious place to start.
  • Networks. Prison peers in Italy, work colleagues in the Finnish paper. Both find sanctions travel.

What you should be able to do afterwards

  • Explain in two sentences why the pardon makes the add-on random, and why incapacitation drops out
  • Say why one more month of expected sentence is also one month less in prison, and how the authors try to separate the two
  • State the social multiplier of about 2 and the assumption it rests on
  • Distinguish general deterrence, specific deterrence, incapacitation and peer effects, with one paper for each
