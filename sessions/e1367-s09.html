GRAD-E1367 · Economics of Media · Session 9

Hate speech

Wednesday 4 November, 12:00–14:00, room 2.32. Online toxicity and its consequences: one field experiment on engagement, one quasi-experiment on offline violence.

Required reading

  • Beknazar-Yuzbashev, Jiménez-Durán, McCrosky and Stalinski (2026), Toxic Content and User Engagement on Social Media: Evidence from a Field Experiment, working paper. Debate paper this session; Felipe is one of the critics.
  • Müller and Schwarz (2021), Fanning the Flames of Hate: Social Media and Hate Crime, JEEA 19(4), 2131–2167.

What connects them

Beknazar-Yuzbashev et al. randomly hide toxic posts and comments for six weeks in volunteers' browsers and measure what happens to engagement on Facebook, Twitter and YouTube. The outcome stays online.

Müller and Schwarz link anti-refugee posts on the AfD's Facebook page to attacks on refugees in German municipalities, 2015–2017, and use Facebook and local internet outages to argue the link runs through social media. In the published text, a one standard deviation higher local right-wing Facebook use goes with a 10% higher weekly probability of an incident, and incidents are about 53% less likely during a severe local internet outage. The authors note they cannot tell whether social media shifts the timing of attacks or adds to their number.

One paper randomises individual exposure but cannot see offline harm; the other sees offline harm but not what individuals read. Germany's 2017 NetzDG law is the policy thread between them.

Reading pass

Müller and Schwarz: introduction (pp. 2131–2135), empirical strategy and outage results (§3.1–3.3), and the back-of-envelope in §3.7. About 30 minutes.

Questions to bring: does an internet outage isolate social media, or does it also cut other online channels? Does hiding content for one user estimate the same thing as cutting a platform for a whole town?

Reading dossiers on the toxic-content paper, checked against the 18 May 2026 draft.
