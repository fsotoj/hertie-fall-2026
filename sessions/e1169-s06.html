Re-integration: expected and unexpected effects

Wednesday 7 October 2026, 10:00–12:00, room 2.32. Christian Traxler. Seminar session: two required papers whose results run against intuition. In Norway a prison spell helps some defendants back into work; in New Jersey and New York City a policy meant to help ex-offenders into work widens the racial gap in callbacks.

The reading

Two required papers. The syllabus frames the session as the expected and unexpected effects of re-integration policies.

Bhuller, Dahl, Løken and Mogstad: is prison criminogenic or preventive?

Random judges in Norway, and a result that depends on whether you had a job.

Design. Norwegian law assigns criminal cases to judges at random within a court, and judges differ in how often they send defendants to prison. The instrument is a judge's incarceration rate in his or her other cases. A judge with a 10 point higher rate raises the chance of a prison sentence by about 5 points. Two defendants in the same court and year differ only in the judge they drew. There is no plea bargaining, which removes a classic problem of the US versions of this design.

Data. Linked registers on crime and work for the whole population; 33,548 non-confession cases from 2005 to 2009. Prison spells are short: about six months actually served, almost nine in ten under a year.

Result. Incarceration lowers the probability of being charged again within five years by 29 percentage points and cuts charges by 11 (published abstract). Against a complier baseline of about 73%, that is roughly a 40% reduction, with a wide confidence interval. It is not just incapacitation: almost nobody is still inside after two years, yet reoffending between months 25 and 60 still falls by about 25 points. Ordinary regressions give the opposite sign, which the authors read as selection.

The real message is the split. Defendants not working before prison take up job training, raise their employment by 36 points and cut reoffending by 43 points. Defendants who had a job, about half the sample, commit no less crime and lose 30 points of employment, mostly by losing the job they had.

Where to push. Whose effect is this? Compliers in a system that spends heavily per inmate, keeps one prisoner per cell, runs open prisons and offers training. The same design in Texas found prison raises recidivism. So the paper shows that Norwegian prison can be preventive, not that prison is. Fallbacks: charges are not crimes, and a strict judge is also strict on fines and probation, which the exclusion restriction has to rule out.

Agan and Starr: does hiding criminal records help black men get jobs?

An audit experiment wrapped around a policy change.

Design. About 15,000 fictitious online applications from young men to entry-level jobs in New Jersey and New York City, before and after each adopted Ban the Box in 2015. Race (through names) and a felony conviction are randomised. Whether an employer asked about records was not: chains chose it. The policy effect therefore comes from a triple difference, comparing how the white-black callback gap changed at employers that had to remove the box with employers that never had one.

Result, from the published abstract. Records matter: employers that asked were 63% more likely to call back an applicant without one. And Ban the Box widens the racial gap: at affected employers, white applicants got 7% more callbacks than similar black applicants before the law and 43% more after. The authors' reading is that employers rely on exaggerated impressions of racial differences in conviction rates.

Where to push. The headline is about who loses, black applicants without a record, not about the policy's total effect. What happens to black applicants with records, the group the law targets, and is the net effect on black applicants as a whole positive or negative? Fallbacks: callbacks are not hires, since the record check still comes later; and names signal class as well as race.

How the two fit together

  • Two halves of re-integration. What the prison spell does to later crime and work, and what the labour market does to people once they are out.
  • Norway is already a "no box" labour market. Bhuller et al. note that Norwegian employers usually do not ask about records on applications, and short spells leave gaps of months, not years. If hiding records triggers racial discrimination in the US, why does it not undermine re-employment in Norway?
  • Callbacks versus jobs. Agan and Starr measure callbacks in an experiment; the optional Doleac and Hansen paper measures employment in survey data. Same sign, different outcome and design.
  • Continuity with last week. The Finnish financial-crime paper and this one both use random judges in a Nordic country and both find prison deters someone. The question is always who.

What you should be able to do afterwards

  • Explain why random judges plus differences in stringency give an instrument, and name relevance, exclusion and monotonicity
  • Say why the effect after month 24 separates prison's lasting effect from incapacitation
  • State the split by prior employment and what it implies for policy
  • Explain statistical discrimination, and why removing information can increase it
  • Say what Agan and Starr's triple difference compares, and what has to hold for it
