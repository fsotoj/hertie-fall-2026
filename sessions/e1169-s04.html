Cheng and Long: the French Quarter Task Force

Visible policing, and then the question of whether management changes what the same officers deliver.

The setting. In March 2015 a private resident of the French Quarter in New Orleans funded off-duty city police officers to patrol the Quarter on all-terrain vehicles around the clock, reachable through a smartphone app, and managed them like a private business: GPS tracking and dismissal of underperformers. Press and secondary summaries (not verified against the paper) say he ran it only until late June 2015, after which a public body took over and largely dropped those practices.

Design. Difference-in-differences: the Quarter against other New Orleans neighbourhoods, before and after the launch (the comparison group is from secondary summaries). The second result uses the change in management within the program.

Results, from the abstract. The task force reduced robberies by 37.4%, aggravated assaults by 16.9% and thefts by 13%. More monitoring and performance incentives reduced robberies by 22.12 and aggravated assaults by 5.56 per quarter. Those last two are counts, so they need the paper's baseline before they can be compared with the percentages.

Where to push. The management result. If the private regime lasted about three months, "more monitoring against less" is also spring against summer, novelty against routine and one funder against another, all in a single treated area. Ask how many quarters of the private regime the data contain, and what separates the management effect from the program simply wearing off. Displacement into neighbouring districts and the effect of the app on crime reporting are the two fallback questions.

Mastrobuoni: predictive policing in Milan

The police stay fixed; what changes is the information they patrol with.

The technology. KeyCrime, built by a Milan police officer and in use from 2008, links commercial robberies into sequences by the same group and predicts that group's next target type, neighbourhood, day and time. It predicts offenders, not places, which sets it apart from the hot-spot software most of the policy debate is about.

Design. Milan has two police forces. The Polizia had KeyCrime, the Carabinieri did not, and the city's three sectors rotate between them every six hours, so which force handles a given robbery is close to random. Because the forces might simply differ in skill, three second differences net that out: a group's first robbery (nothing yet to predict from), robberies on the same day as the previous one (victims are interviewed the next day, so the prediction has not been updated), and the 2010 order that forced the Polizia to share its reports.

Results, from the manuscript. Data: all 2,164 commercial robberies in Milan, 2008 to mid-2011. No gap between the forces on first robberies. On later robberies the Polizia clears about 10 percentage points more, on a Carabinieri base near 5.6%: almost three times as often. The difference-in-differences is 7.8 points (significant only at 10%) using first against later robberies, and 13.3 points using same-day against later-day robberies. The gap closes once reports are shared. Since a serial group keeps robbing until caught, expected robberies per group are about one over the clearance rate: 17.8 without the software, 6.4 with it. The author puts the avoided direct losses near €2.5 million against under €100,000 a year in staff.

Where to push. The treated force builds the treatment variable: the Polizia decides which robberies belong to a sequence, and so which count as "later". The author answers with the same-day and data-sharing designs, which do not use the sequence count; whether they fully escape it, when they still rest on Polizia linkages, is the sharp question. Two fallbacks: each estimate on its own is imprecise (the strength is that three designs agree), and serial professional robbers caught on CCTV are close to the best case for prediction, so the result says little about place-based tools and the bias concerns from session 3.

How the two fit together

  • Two jobs of the police. Cheng and Long change visible presence and measure crime: mostly deterrence. Mastrobuoni changes information and measures arrests: the police as apprehension agents, with the crime effect coming through incapacitation.
  • Management as a technology. GPS and dismissal in New Orleans, data and prediction in Milan. Both ask whether the same officers can produce more.
  • One treated place each. Milan escapes it through random rotation inside the city; the French Quarter design, as far as the abstract shows, has no such option. That is the shortest honest comparison of the two.

What you should be able to do afterwards

  • State each paper's comparison in one sentence: which group against which, which period against which
  • Explain why a six-hour sector rotation makes the handling force as good as random, and why a second difference is still needed
  • Turn a clearance rate into expected robberies per serial group, and say why that is incapacitation rather than deterrence
  • Name what could pass for a management effect in the French Quarter
  • Say what the Milan result does and does not tell you about hot-spot prediction and biased policing
