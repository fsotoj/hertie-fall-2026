Djourelova, Durante and Martin: when the classifieds leave

The question. Classified ads were 40% of US newspapers' advertising revenue in 2000. When Craigslist took that business, what happened to newsrooms, to political coverage, to readers, and to congressional elections?

Why Craigslist. It carries no news and no display ads, so it hits the classifieds business and nothing else. It entered US counties in a staggered way between 1995 and 2009. And it was barely run for profit, which weakens the worry that it picked markets where newspapers were already weak: once population and internet quality are controlled for, newspaper characteristics do not predict when it arrived.

The identification. Two layers of difference-in-differences on hand-digitized staff lists of more than 1,500 dailies, 1995–2010. First, papers in counties Craigslist entered against those it had not yet entered. Second, and preferred: within those markets, papers that had a dedicated classified ad manager in 2000 (and so depended more on classifieds) against those that did not. The toughest check compares two papers in the same county and year.

  • Newsrooms: exposed papers lose about 3 jobs, 14% of the mean. Political editors are cut; sports and entertainment editors are not.
  • Content: the politics topic weight falls 8%, and articles naming local congressional candidates fall 12%, mostly around primaries.
  • Readers: circulation falls about 4%, and the fall comes from people who read the paper for news, not for the classifieds. No sign they moved to news online.
  • Elections: no effect on turnout, but split-ticket voting falls about 13%, and extreme candidates enter and win primaries more often (+9.5 points in the chance an extremist runs). The authors themselves downgrade the result on extremists winning general elections.

The weak point. The newsroom and content results are well identified. The political results travel from county-level treatment to district-level outcomes (about 1,200 counties in 440 districts), and cannot show that the voters who changed behaviour are the readers who lost coverage. The classified-manager proxy also correlates with newspaper size, which the authors address with three separate checks.

The idea to take away. Classified profits cross-subsidised political reporting that neither readers nor advertisers paid for directly. Take the subsidy away and accountability reporting goes first.

Wochner: when the advertiser is the government

From the abstract only. Magnitudes and design to be read on Moodle.

The question. Do discretionary advertising purchases by Austrian ministries buy newspaper coverage of the minister who controls the budget, and does that coverage move voters?

  • Ministry ad spending raises both the quantity and the positivity of a newspaper's coverage of the corresponding minister.
  • The effects are immediate, hold beyond ideological alignment between minister and paper, and are stronger where newspapers depend more on public funds.
  • Voting intentions move asymmetrically: the minister's base consolidates and opponents react against, which amplifies polarisation.

The setting. Austria's Media Transparency Act has required public bodies to report their advertising per outlet since 2012, and the 2021 affair around Sebastian Kurz's ministry and a tabloid made ads-for-coverage a national scandal. Whether and how the paper uses either is for the Moodle copy to say.

Questions to bring. Ministries advertise when they have something to announce, which is also when the minister is in the news: how does the paper separate paid coverage from an information campaign that is news in itself? How is positivity measured? And how is a voter's exposure to a particular newspaper observed? The heterogeneity by dependence on public money is the part of the abstract hardest to explain away.

Why the two are assigned together

The same revenue line, removed in one case and weaponised in the other.

The discussion question worth having ready: historians of the press argue that advertising is what freed newspapers from parties. Craigslist shows what happens when that money goes; Wochner shows what happens when the replacement advertiser is the state. Which is worse for voters, and what does that imply for how public support for news should be designed?

What you should be able to do afterwards

  • Explain why Craigslist is a cleaner shock than "the internet", and what the classified-manager comparison adds
  • State the chain with one number per link: jobs, political coverage, circulation, turnout, split-ticket voting
  • Say why the fall in readership among news readers, not classified readers, points to content as the cause
  • Say which political result the authors themselves treat with caution
  • State Wochner's claim and the identification threat any ads-for-coverage design has to answer
