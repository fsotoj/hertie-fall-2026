Seven questions to ask of a paper

A fixed order for reading a paper, for the seminars that grade you on doing it out loud: the two Economics of Media debates and the Economics of Crime presentation.

Stakes What is the question, and what would change if the answer came out the other way? A paper worth its length has a decision hanging off it. If you cannot name the decision, you are reading a summary of the abstract rather than the paper. This is also the answer to the first thing a seminar asks.
Variation Where does the variation come from, and who assigned it? Someone or something decided who got treated: a randomiser, a legislature, a redistricting commission, a market boundary, an accident. Name that agent. Almost every objection you will ever raise is really an objection to it.
Comparison What exactly is being compared with what? Write the two groups, or the two periods, in your own words before looking at a table. Doing this first is what makes a regression table readable, because every coefficient is then an answer to a question you already hold.
Assumption What has to be true for the estimate to be causal, and what would break it? State it as something that could be false: parallel trends, exclusion, continuity at the cutoff. Then name the concrete story under which it fails. A critique that cannot say what the world looks like when the assumption breaks is not yet a critique.
Magnitude How big is the effect in units you can feel? Relative to the control mean, per euro spent, per additional officer, per percentage point of coverage. Significance says the effect is not zero, which is rarely the interesting claim. A well-identified estimate too small to matter is a real finding and should be read as one.
Whom Who does this estimate apply to? Rarely everyone. It is the compliers, or the districts near the threshold, or Delhi councillors in high-slum wards. External validity is not a closing caveat: it decides whether the paper speaks to the policy question you actually care about.
Residual What does the paper not rule out, and what would settle it? Name the strongest surviving alternative explanation and the test that would separate it from the paper's own. This is the hardest of the seven and the one that produces something to say in a room where everyone has read the same pages.

The order matters: four cannot be answered before two and three are, and seven is only reachable once four is. If time runs out, four and seven are the two that a seminar actually rewards, and the two a presentation is graded on.
