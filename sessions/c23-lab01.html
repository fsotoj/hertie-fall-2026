Simulating the birthday problem

Wednesday 2 September 2026, 18:00–20:00, room 3.30. Python, NumPy and pandas, with the session 1 probability material turned into simulations: birthdays, dice, and the drunken passenger. The handout and a skeleton notebook are in the course materials repository.

Set up before you arrive, not in the room

The handout opens with an environment, and installing one during a two-hour lab costs a quarter of it.

The lab runs on Python, not R. That is the first thing to get straight if you have been living in the Data Science course, which is R throughout. The handout uses uv to manage both the interpreter and the packages: install uv, run uv init to create a pyproject.toml, then uv add numpy pandas matplotlib jupyter, and open the notebook with uv run jupyter lab or in VS Code. Without uv, Python 3.10 or newer and the same four packages through pip do the same job.

Every cell in the notebook imports from that set: numpy, pandas, matplotlib.pyplot, and factorial and comb from the standard library's math.

What the lab actually asks

Six blocks, moving from syntax to two genuinely interesting problems.

Warm-up, exercises 1 to 3. Arrays and data frames, then indexing and summarising: mean, min, max, sum, and the proportion of elements above a threshold. Then the probability primitives: factorial, comb, and NumPy's random generator for sampling with and without replacement, with your own probabilities, rolling one die, two dice, and a loaded die. It closes on seeding, and the question of what a seed actually does.

Simulation, exercise 4: the birthday problem. One simulated class of 35 people first, then a hundred thousand of them, counting how many contain a repeat and reading the proportion as the frequentist probability. The last step compares that against the closed form derived in the lecture. This is the session's real content: the same number arrived at twice, once by counting and once by experiment.

Plotting, exercise 5. Rebuild the birthday curve for classes of 1 to 100 people, into a two-column data frame, then a scatter plot with axis labels, a title, and a red horizontal line at 0.5 so the crossing point is visible.

Exercise 6: the drunken passenger. A hundred passengers board a hundred-seat plane; the first sits at random, and everyone after takes their own seat if it is free and a random free one otherwise. What is the probability the last passenger gets their assigned seat? The lab builds it up in steps, wraps it in a function, runs it ten thousand times, then generalises to any number of passengers to show the answer does not depend on it. Part g asks for the argument rather than the simulation, and points at the solution to exercise 59 in Blitzstein and Hwang.

Worth knowing the answer to 6 before you start coding, because the simulation is much easier to debug when you know what it should converge to. Two seats matter and they are symmetric.

What you should be able to do afterwards

  • Sample with and without replacement, with custom probabilities, and say when each applies

  • Recover the birthday formula and check it against the simulation
  • Say why the drunken passenger answer is one half, without running anything
