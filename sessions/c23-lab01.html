What the lab is for

The syllabus describes the course as lectures that carry the main content plus small-group lab sessions that reinforce and extend the material, with hands-on tutorials alongside the regular assignments and the in-class quizzes. The labs are not optional in the syllabus's own framing: it asks for consistent weekly effort across readings, assignments and regular attendance of both the lecture and the lab.

This one follows session 1 by a day, so it works over probability theory: the session 1 primer has the material. Sample spaces and events as sets, the naive definition and why it is called naive, the four counting cases from ordered or unordered against with or without replacement, the axioms, conditioning, Bayes with a partition, independence against conditional independence, and Monty Hall as the demonstration that intuition about conditioning is unreliable.

Worth arriving with a specific question rather than a general one. The parts of session 1 that survive contact with exercises are the two-by-two counting table and the difference between P(A|B) and P(B|A); those are where a worked example pays.

What this lab feeds into

Two deadlines follow it closely enough to be worth raising tomorrow.

  • Problem set 1, released Tuesday 8 September, due Sunday 20 September at 23:59. Three problem sets over the semester, 5% each. Solutions must be typed, LaTeX strongly recommended but not required, and every step shown: the syllabus asks to be able to trace the whole process and to run any submitted code unchanged.
  • Small groups are assigned for the problem sets, with one solution set handed in per group. Who is in which group is not in any document here, so it is a question for the lab.
  • Quiz 1 is session 3, Tuesday 15 September, at the start of the lecture, pen and paper. All five quizzes count with no lowest score dropped, so the lab in the week before it is the cheapest place to find out what you cannot yet do.
