Chapter 1 — what probability is, and how to count

The language first

An experiment has a sample space: every outcome that could happen. An event is any subset of it. Once events are sets, the logical connectives become set operations, so "A and B" is intersection, "A or B" is union, "not A" is complement. Most of the early difficulty in this course is translating an English sentence into that notation, not the arithmetic that follows.

The naive definition, and why it is called that

If every outcome is equally likely, the probability of an event is the count of favourable outcomes over the count of possible ones. The name is doing real work: it assumes equal likelihood and a finite sample space, and both assumptions fail constantly in practice. The chapter uses it as a stepping stone, not as a definition to keep.

Counting, because the naive definition needs it

The multiplication rule chains independent choices. From it come permutations, which are ordered arrangements, and combinations, which are unordered selections written as the binomial coefficient. The organising idea is a two-by-two table: sampling is ordered or unordered, and with or without replacement. Four formulas, but one structure rather than four things to memorise.

Story proofs

An argument that establishes an identity by describing two ways of counting the same set, instead of manipulating algebra. This is the house style of the course and it is what the quizzes reward. C(n,k) = C(n,n-k) stops needing a derivation once you say that choosing which k to include is the same act as choosing which n−k to leave out.

The axioms

The non-naive definition drops the equally-likely assumption and keeps three rules: probabilities are non-negative, the whole sample space has probability 1, and the probability of a union of disjoint events is the sum of their probabilities. Everything else, the complement rule and inclusion–exclusion included, is derived from those three.

Chapter 2 — conditioning, which is the actual subject

Conditional probability is the probability of A given that B occurred, defined as the joint probability divided by P(B). The intuition worth carrying: conditioning shrinks the sample space to B and renormalises so the total is 1 again. It is not a modification of A, it is a change of universe.

Bayes' rule inverts the conditioning, turning P(A|B) into P(B|A) with the marginals as the correction factor. The law of total probability builds P(B) by splitting the sample space into cases and summing the conditional probabilities, each weighted by how likely its case is. In practice the two are used together: partition, then invert.

Independence means conditioning changes nothing, so P(A|B) = P(A). That is a strong claim about the world rather than a convenience, and the chapter is careful to separate it from conditional independence, where A and B are independent given C but not independent on their own. The distinction returns later, in Bayesian analysis and in anything causal.

The traps the chapter dwells on are the ones that matter for policy work: confusing P(A|B) with P(B|A), and neglecting the base rate. A test that is 99% accurate for a condition affecting one person in ten thousand still produces mostly false positives. That is Bayes' rule doing the work, and it is the single most transferable thing in the session.

Monty Hall

Called out separately because it sits past the assigned page range, and because it is the standard demonstration that intuition about conditioning is unreliable. The whole result turns on whether the host's choice of door carries information. Read it slowly and make sure you can state precisely what the host knows and how that changes the conditioning. The answer is the uninteresting part next to that step.

What you should be able to do afterwards

  • Translate a word problem into a sample space and events
  • Choose between the four counting cases and justify which one applies
  • State the three axioms and derive the complement rule from them
  • Apply Bayes' rule with a partition, and explain a base-rate error to someone else
  • Say why the naive definition fails when outcomes are not equally likely
