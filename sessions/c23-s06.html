What the deck covers

Six parts, each one a short step from the last.

Limit theorems (slides 4–11). The sample mean has mean μ and variance σ²/n, so its error shrinks like 1/√n: four times the data halves the error. The law of large numbers says it settles on μ, by swamping rather than by compensation (after ten heads the next toss is still a coin flip). The central limit theorem says the standardised mean becomes Normal whatever the data look like, provided the mean and the variance are finite. A Binomial is a sum of Bernoullis, so it is approximately N(np, np(1 − p)).

The Beta distribution (12–18). A black box pays out 14 times in 41. Fix that data and let the success probability p vary: the Binomial probability becomes a curve in p, the likelihood, and its shape is a Beta(15, 28). The parameters are one more than the successes and one more than the failures.

The likelihood (19–24). The joint density of the data, read as a function of the parameter with the data held fixed. It need not integrate to one, constants do not move its peak, and taking logs turns the product into a sum without moving the peak either. The recipe: write L, take the log, set the derivative to zero, check the second derivative is negative.

Three worked cases (25–53). A goalkeeper's saves (Binomial): the estimate is total saves over total shots, 8/20 in the four-game example, and not the average of the per-game rates when games have different numbers of shots. Shots per game (Poisson): the sample mean, 4. Normal data with both parameters unknown: the sample mean, and a variance that divides by n rather than n − 1. In every case the maximum likelihood estimate turns out to be the estimate you would have guessed.

  • Match a Binomial likelihood to a Beta(k + 1, n − k + 1) shape
  • Run the four-step recipe for the Binomial, the Poisson and the Normal
