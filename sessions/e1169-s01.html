Introduction, and why crime trends reversed

Wednesday 2 September 2026, 10:00–12:00, room 2.32. Christian Traxler. One required paper, 39 journal pages, on Moodle. This session and the next are the only two in lecture format: from session 3 the course is seminar-style discussion of papers.

The reading

The syllabus lists one required paper for the session, and four optional ones that all circle the same question from different angles.

Buonanno, P., F. Drago, R. Galbiati and G. Zanella (2011), "Crime in Europe and the United States: dissecting the 'reversal of misfortunes'", Economic Policy 26(67), pp. 347–385.

Optional: Levitt (2004) on why crime fell in the 1990s, four factors that explain it and six that do not; Lofstrom and Raphael (2016) on crime, the criminal justice system and inequality; Jaitman (2017) on the cost of murder in Latin America; Rozo (2018) on the cost of violent crime in Colombia.

What the paper sets up

Summarised from the published abstract. The paper itself is on Moodle, so check the argument below against it rather than the other way round.

The starting fact is a reversal. Today both property crime and violent crime are more widespread in Europe than in the United States; thirty years earlier the ranking was the other way round. Homicide is the exception, and it stays an exception throughout: the United States remains the more lethal place. Any summary that drops that caveat has stated the result wrong.

The dissection is a decomposition exercise on time series from 1970 to 2008, seven European countries against the United States. Five candidate drivers are put to it: demographic change, incarceration, abortion, unemployment and immigration. Each is a story that was made to carry the American decline at some point in the literature, and the point of running them on both sides of the Atlantic is to see which survive the comparison.

Read it as the empirical puzzle the rest of the course answers, not as the answer. The identification problem is visible on the surface here: aggregate national time series, a handful of countries, five explanations that move together with each other and with everything else. That is the setting the course spends twelve sessions escaping, via the quasi-experimental designs the syllabus names in the general readings.

The method refresher the syllabus expects

Statistics 2 (causal inference) is a stated prerequisite. The syllabus asks for a refresher on OLS, on what endogeneity does to it (omitted variables, selection, reverse causation), and on the identification strategies that recur in every session: difference-in-differences, instrumental variables, regression discontinuity. Its non-technical recommendation is Gertler et al. (2016), Impact Evaluation in Practice, chapters 1 and 3–7, free from the World Bank. Angrist and Pischke, Mostly Harmless Econometrics, sections 1–5, is the more advanced option.

What this session settles, beyond the reading

Course organisation happens here, and it fixes dates that matter later.

  • Which paper you present, and in which session. Each student gets a different one, and the presentation is 45% of the grade: roughly 15 minutes, topic first, then the main idea, the method and the key results.
  • The slide deadline follows from that date. Slides as PDF by email to Traxler the Tuesday evening before the presentation; they can still be adjusted before speaking on the Wednesday, and the final version goes to Moodle afterwards.
  • The resident expert role. For several sessions, some students read past the required list and act as the reference for everyone else. It is part of the 15% participation grade, not a separate assignment.
  • Attendance. Two absences out of twelve, stated flatly here with no "unless assignments are affected" qualifier, and Traxler asks to be told by email in advance.
  • Office hours. Mondays 10:00–12:00, marked "t.b.c." in the syllabus, booked through Calendly.

What you should be able to do afterwards

  • State the reversal precisely, including the homicide exception
  • Name the five candidate drivers and say how each would show up in the data
  • Say what a cross-country time-series decomposition can identify and what it cannot
  • Explain why the course spends its first two sessions on method before topics
  • Know your presentation date, and therefore your slide deadline
Looking ahead

Session 2, Wednesday 9 September, is the economic theory of crime: Becker's rational choice model and general deterrence, confronted with specific deterrence, then incapacitation and displacement. The only required reading is the lecture slides, which is unusual for this course and worth not misreading as "no preparation".

The optional list there is the canon: Becker (1968), Chalfin and McCrary (2017) chapters 1 and 2 for the deterrence review, Draca, Machin and Witt (2011) on the July 2005 London attacks, Dušek and Traxler (2022), and Levitt (1997) on electoral cycles in police hiring.
