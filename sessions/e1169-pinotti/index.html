Introduction

Does legal status itself reduce immigrant crime?

  • Question. Irregular immigrants: 80% of immigrants arrested for serious crimes in Italy (2006), under 20% of the foreign population. Selection or causal effect of status?
  • Experiment. Italy's click days: fixed permit quotas, first come first served. A few minutes decide who gets legal status.

Background 1/2

Click days: a race against an unknown cutoff

  • Annual quota per province × permit type: 170,000 permits, 610,239 applications for 2008
  • Employers apply online from 08:00; processed in order of arrival until the quota runs out
  • 1,580 lotteries; median cutoff 08:39; nobody knows it in advance

Background 2/2

Who applies, and why status should matter

  • Type A: domestic and care workers for families. 46% of male applicants in Lombardy, against 2.5% of employed immigrant men (ISMU). Shared surname with sponsor in 21%: many fictitious job offers
  • Type B: firm employees; sponsors checked against business registries
  • Mechanism (Becker): legal status raises legitimate earnings, so it raises the opportunity cost of crime
  • Institutions: a de facto regularisation (most applicants already in Italy); deportation risk only about 5% a year, so status is about the labour market

  • Equation (2) in the paper, limits as the bandwidth goes to zero; I simplified the notation on the slide.
  • Why fuzzy: before the cutoff, incomplete or fraudulent files are rejected (more for type B); after it, some late applicants are accepted when earlier ones fail. First stage 0.459 overall, 0.610 type A, 0.372 type B.

  • If asked about manipulation: sponsors can click early, but cannot know where the cutoff falls. Density (McCrary) and covariates smooth (Figs 4-5).
  • Estimated cutoffs: if Andrews over-detects breaks, it includes lotteries with no jump, which biases toward zero.

Threats to identification 1/3

Where were the applicants?

Presence in Italy before click days
  • Applicants are supposed to be abroad; some may really be
  • Only the legalised could then enter Italy, which would raise their measured crime
  • So the estimate is a lower bound
Collecting the entry visa
  • Legalised applicants must go home, collect a visa, re-enter
  • Time abroad mechanically lowers crime in Italy
  • To explain the effect they would need more than six months abroad (8.7 for type A)
Testable: if the visa trip drives it, the effect should be larger for far countries and vanish late in 2008. → next slide

Measurement error works against the result

  • Three patterns, one story: type A not B; economic not violent; foreign not native sponsor. Each fits the labour-market channel; none tests it directly.
  • Careful: foreign −0.015 vs native −0.008 are not shown to be significantly different from each other (native s.e. 0.007). Say "driven by", as the paper does, not "only".

Conclusions

Legalisation lowers crime, most where job prospects are worst

Takeaways
  • Legal status cuts serious crime by 1.3 pp among type A, about 0.6 pp overall
  • Clean design: an unknown cutoff makes status as good as random
  • Driven by economic crime: an opportunity-cost story
  • Policy: amnesty has a crime dividend, largest for the group a demand-driven quota is designed to exclude
Limits
  • One year only: the control group is legalised in 2009
  • Behaviour or exit? No data on rejected applicants leaving
  • External validity: men, near the cutoff, Italy with about 5% deportation risk; the US differs
  • Partial equilibrium: applicants' own crime, not total crime or future inflows
