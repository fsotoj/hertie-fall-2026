Session 8 · required reading

The mirror image: cutting legitimate income raises crime

Pinotti (2017)
Italy, click days 2007
  • Status granted by a few minutes
  • Cutoff: application time
  • Legal work opportunities up
  • Serious crime down, economic crimes only
↔
Andersen, Dustmann and Landersø (2019)
Denmark, "Start Aid", July 2002
  • Benefits cut by up to 50% for refugees granted residence after 1 July
  • Cutoff: date of residence permit
  • Employment up in the short run only; poverty up
  • Subsistence crime (theft, shoplifting) up
Opposite shocks to legitimate income, opposite effects on crime, both in economically motivated offences: the opportunity-cost channel from two directions.

Session 8 · optional readings 1/2

Do immigrants raise crime, and does status matter?

Bianchi, Buonanno and Pinotti (2012)
JEEA 10(6) · Italian provinces, IV from immigration to other destinations
Immigration raises robberies only; no effect on the total crime rate.
The aggregate question that Pinotti narrows to individual behaviour
Mastrobuoni and Pinotti (2015)
AEJ: Applied 7(2) · EU enlargement 2007, pardoned inmates
Recidivism fell from 5.8% to 2.3% over six months for inmates from the new member states.
Same author: legal status through EU membership instead of click days
Chalfin (2015)
AER P&P 105(5) · US cities, Mexican fertility rates as instrument
Long-run effect of Mexican immigration on city crime. Results not verified; his earlier rainfall-IV paper found mixed effects, not a clean null.
Aggregate US counterpart
Also optional: Gertler et al. (2016), Impact Evaluation in Practice, ch. 6, regression discontinuity recap.

Notes

  • Bianchi et al.: the 2008 working paper also reported murders and thefts; the published abstract keeps robberies only.

Session 8 · optional readings 2/2

Other channels, other policies, other direction

Damm and Dustmann (2014)
AER 104(6) · Danish refugee dispersal, quasi-random placement
More young convicted offenders in the neighbourhood → more later convictions for boys, mainly violent.
Social interaction, not labour market: the session title's second channel
Hines and Peri (2019)
IZA DP 12413 · US Secure Communities, 2008-13
Deportations rose, crime did not fall; no gain in police clearance rates.
Enforcement as the alternative to legalisation. A contrast, not a like-for-like comparison
Entorf and Lange (2019)
IZA DP 12229 · German counties, asylum influx 2014-15
Hate crime against refugees highest where few foreign-born lived before and many arrived; strongest in the East.
Crime against migrants: the other direction

Notes

  • Hines and Peri is the one to have ready if Traxler asks "deportation or legalisation?". Pinotti's own setting has about 5% annual deportation risk.
  • Entorf and Lange published 2023 in Regional Science and Urban Economics under a slightly different title.
Further literature 1/3

Legal status and crime in other settings

Baker (2015)
AER P&P 105(5) · IRCA 1986, US counties, IV
IRCA legalisation reduced crime across US counties (as Pinotti describes it).
Macro counterpart of Pinotti's micro design
Freedman, Owens and Bohn (2018)
AEJ: Policy 10(2) · IRCA employer sanctions, San Antonio, triple differences
Felony charges rose for those shut out of legal work once the amnesty window closed; financially motivated crime.
The flip side: losing work access raises crime
Fasani (2018)
J. Economic Geography 18(4) · Italian amnesties 1990-2005, regional IV
Non-EU immigrant crime falls the year after an amnesty, but the effect is small and not persistent.
The only Italian evidence on persistence, and it cuts against a long-run reading of Pinotti
B5Backup

Notes

  • Fasani (2018) is the best answer to "Pinotti only has one year": the regional evidence says the drop fades.
  • Baker's abstract was not retrieved; the summary comes from Pinotti's own literature review.
Further literature 2/3

The labour-market channel Pinotti cannot measure

Devillanova, Fasani and Frattini (2018)
ILR Review · Italy 2002 amnesty, arrival-date cutoff
The prospect of legal status raised employment of eligible undocumented immigrants.
Same country, similar design: fills Pinotti's missing employment data
Couttenier, Petrencu, Rohner and Thoenig (2019)
AER · Swiss asylum seekers, federal allocation
Childhood conflict exposure raises violent crime 35%; labour market access removes about two-thirds.
Most direct evidence that work access lowers migrant crime
Monras, Vázquez-Grenno and Elias (2025)
J. Labor Economics 43(3) · Spain 2005, about 600,000 legalised
Formal employment up; payroll tax about €4,000 more per person; no magnet effect.
Legalisation moves people into formal work
Bell, Fasani and Machin (2013)
REStat 95(4) · UK asylum wave vs A8 wave
Asylum wave raised property crime modestly, A8 wave did not; no violent crime effect.
Same economic-vs-violent split as Pinotti's Table 6
Fasani, Frattini and Minale (2021)
JEEA 19(5) · European employment bans for asylum seekers, 1985-2012
A ban at arrival cuts refugee employment by 15%, for up to 10 years.
The cost of withholding work rights
B6Backup

Notes

  • Couttenier et al.: earlier drafts (Couttenier, Preotu, ...) said 40% and that labour access removed "all" of the effect. Cite the AER numbers: 35%, two-thirds.
Further literature 3/3

Latin America: Venezuelan migration

Ibáñez et al., "Life out of the Shadows"
JEEA · Colombia's PEP amnesty, fuzzy RD on the June 2018 registration cutoff
PEP holders: consumption +18%, income +24.5%, better access to safety nets and financial services. A summary reports no effect on crimes against Colombians.
Closest design to Pinotti outside Italy: a cutoff that nobody anticipated
Ajzenman, Dominguez and Undurraga (2023)
AEJ: Applied 15(4) · Chile, municipalities, shift-share IV
Immigration had no effect on crime or victimisation, but raised crime concern and spending on home security.
Perceptions vs reality: why the legal/illegal distinction matters politically

Notes

  • Colombia paper: its own crime estimates were not seen; summaries disagree on formalisation. Author list taken from the working paper.
