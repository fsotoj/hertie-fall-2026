What chapter 3 does

Session 1 was about events, which are sets. This chapter replaces them with numbers, and that shift is the whole point of it.

A random variable is a function, not a variable. It maps each outcome in the sample space to a number. That definition sounds like bookkeeping and is not: it is what lets you do arithmetic on uncertainty, and everything later in the course — expectation, variance, estimators, loss functions — depends on it.

The distribution is separate from the variable. Two random variables defined on completely different experiments can share a distribution. The probability mass function gives the probability of each value, and the chapter is careful that the PMF describes the distribution rather than the variable itself. Beginners routinely collapse the two.

Then the named distributions, each introduced by the story that generates it rather than by its formula: Bernoulli, a single trial; Binomial, a count of successes in independent trials with the same probability; Hypergeometric, the same count when sampling without replacement, so the trials are not independent; and the discrete uniform. The book's method is to make the story primary, so that you recognise which distribution applies from the setup instead of matching formulas.

Cumulative distribution functions come next, giving the probability of being at most some value. The CDF is the tool that survives into continuous random variables in session 4 or 5, where the PMF does not exist.

The chapter closes on functions of random variables and independence of random variables, which is a stronger condition than the independence of events from session 1, and on the connection between the Binomial and the Hypergeometric.

What you should be able to do afterwards

  • Say why a random variable is a function, and what its domain is
  • Distinguish a random variable from its distribution
  • Recognise Bernoulli, Binomial and Hypergeometric from the story, not the formula
  • Explain why sampling without replacement breaks the Binomial assumption
  • Build a CDF from a PMF and say what the CDF is good for that the PMF is not
  • Distinguish independence of events from independence of random variables
