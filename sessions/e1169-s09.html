Private crime protection: does crime fall, or move?

Wednesday 4 November 2026, 10:00-12:00, room 2.32. Christian Traxler. Seminar session, participation only. Two required papers on the same question: in the Netherlands, burglary-proof windows mandated in new homes cut burglary there by about a quarter with no detectable displacement; in Mexico, a tracker sold on advertised Ford models cut their theft by about half, and part of it moved to the same models in other states.

Vollaard and van Ours: mandated burglary-proof homes

The 1999 Dutch Building Code, and whether forcing precaution lowers crime.

Design. From 1999 every new Dutch home needs certified burglary-proof locks, windows and doors. Completion lags the permit by about two years, so homes completed from 2001 on count as treated. Burglary victimisation is compared between homes built 1993-2000 and 2001-2005, with survey-year and province fixed effects and household, home and neighbourhood controls. Treatment is inferred from the construction year, not observed.

Data. The Dutch national victimisation survey, 2005-2008, burglary with loss in the last 24 months, linked to the building register: 9,784 respondents. For displacement to older homes, all 57,422 respondents in homes built before 2001.

Result. Burglary falls by 0.56 percentage points on a 2.15% base, about 26%, from 1.1% to 0.8% a year. The raw difference is almost the same (0.54). Attempts fall much less: the security stops entry more than the decision to try. No significant displacement to older homes nearby or to bicycle theft and theft from cars. About 10,000 burglaries prevented in 2001-2009. Cost about €430 per home, discounted benefit about €460: worth it, narrowly.

Where to push. The treatment is collinear with the age of the home: every treated home is newer than every control. With home age in the regression the estimate is 0.70 with a standard error of 0.60, no longer distinguishable from zero, and the narrow windows around 2001 are not significant either. Fallbacks: 11 province clusters; displacement tests that are not significant but not tight, against a cost-benefit margin of €30 per home.

Gonzalez-Navarro: Lojack on visible Ford models

Same device as in Ayres and Levitt, sold so that thieves can tell who has it.

Setting. Lojack entered Mexico through an exclusive deal with Ford: free in new cars of selected models, in Jalisco, Estado de México, Mexico City and Morelos, with dealers advertising which models carried it. The device is hidden; the model is the signal.

Design. A difference-in-differences on theft counts by model, state, vintage and year stolen, using the staggered entry across models and states. Negative binomial with the vintage stock as exposure, state-by-model fixed effects, age dummies and state trends. Data: insurance-industry theft reports merged with sales by state; 69 model groups, 32 states.

Result. Theft of Lojack-equipped cars falls by 48%. About 18% of that reduction is displaced to the same, unprotected models in states without Lojack. No measurable effect on other models, in Lojack states or elsewhere.

Where to push. Sales are recorded where a car is bought and only insured thefts are counted, so the stock at risk is a noisy map of where cars actually are. If buyers from neighbouring states bought their Lojack Ford in a Lojack state, the mismeasurement falls exactly on the cells that carry the displacement result. Fallbacks: "same model, other state" is not the same as thieves moving; the four Lojack states were chosen by a firm and are high-theft states.

The 2008 working paper, which circulates widely, reported a 55% fall and near-complete displacement, and called the program a net loss for society. The published version is much milder. Quote the 2013 numbers.

How the two fit together

  • Observability decides the sign of the externality. Hidden precaution protects everyone and is under-provided; visible precaution on a subset partly redirects crime to the unprotected and can be over-provided. How a device is sold matters as much as whether it works.
  • Why the Dutch mandate shows little displacement. New homes come in large uniform estates, so the neighbours of a protected home are protected too. That is the authors' argument, not something they test.
  • Mandate against subsidy. Advice, campaigns and subsidies barely move private precaution; building it into the product does, at the cost of protecting homes that would rarely be burgled.
  • Three kinds of displacement: other targets, other places, other crimes. Know which paper tests which, and that a non-significant test is weak evidence of none.

What you should be able to do afterwards

  • Explain when private precaution creates positive and when negative externalities, and what each implies for subsidy, mandate or regulation of how it is sold
  • Say what the Dutch cohort comparison assumes, and why home age is the problem
  • Say what the Mexican difference-in-differences compares, and what the exposure variable does
  • Distinguish deterring the attempt from defeating it, using the Dutch attempted-burglary result
  • Connect to the deterrence and displacement ideas from session 2
