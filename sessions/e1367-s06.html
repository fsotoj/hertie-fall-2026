Martin, Mastrorocco, McCrain and Ornaghi: does it matter which conglomerate buys you?

The question. Sinclair, Nexstar and Gray each own roughly 100 network-affiliated local TV stations; together they control 40% of US news-producing stations. When one of them buys a station, what happens to its local news, its advertising, its audience, and what viewers know about their House member? And is that a conglomerate effect or a Sinclair effect? Earlier work had studied Sinclair alone.

The data. Closed-caption transcripts of nearly every US local newscast (723 stations, 2013–2019), counting mentions of local places and local politicians; Nielsen ad and audience data (695 stations, 2011–2019); acquisition dates from the groups' annual reports; and the CCES survey for political knowledge.

The identification. Staggered difference-in-differences on a station-by-month panel, one effect per owner. The preferred specification adds market-by-month fixed effects, so an acquired station is compared only with its rivals in the same market in the same month, and 2010 ad and ratings profiles are allowed their own trends. Event studies check pre-trends.

  • Content depends on the owner: after a Sinclair purchase, mentions of local places fall by about 1.5 per show (about 10% of the mean); after a Nexstar purchase they rise by 1.0–1.4. Coverage of mayors, state legislators and House members moves the same way, by 30–40% of the mean. Gray changes nothing (Tables 1–2).
  • Advertising does not: ad time per half hour of local news rises 6.3% under Sinclair and 3.9% under Nexstar, close to one extra 30-second spot (Table 3).
  • Viewers stay: no ratings response to Sinclair, enough to rule out declines of 2.2–2.7%; at most a small, fragile rise under Nexstar (Table 4).
  • Knowledge: little effect at the market level (Table 5), although a separate design shows local TV coverage does shape whether people know their representative (Table 6). The authors' reading: an acquisition changes one of about four stations in a market, so the market-level effect is diluted.

The weak point. Three owners, three different content effects, and a theory section that admits it cannot say in advance which force should win: the explanation for Sinclair (political objectives) rests on descriptive evidence about governance and donations, not on a test. The content measure is also "local-ness", not slant or tone.

The idea to take away. If viewers do not notice ownership changes, they do not discipline owners, so the safeguard is market structure: the rule limiting each owner to one of the top four stations in a market.

Couttenier, Hatte, Taylor and Vlachos: a party buys a newspaper

The paper debated today. A short summary only.

In 2010 owners close to the Swiss People's Party (SVP) bought the Basler Zeitung, the dominant daily in Basel; in 2018 they sold it to a non-partisan group. The paper follows one chain through three designs. Content: coverage of SVP-initiated referendums rises about 35% around the vote, with more of it echoing the campaign's own arguments, while referendums the SVP opposed get more coverage but no more aligned coverage (Table 1). Readers: circulation falls 28.8%, most in left-leaning places, but many stay (Table 2). Votes: where the paper was read more before the takeover, the SVP-aligned vote share rises by 0.34 points at average exposure, on platform referendums and not on others, with no pre-trend and no turnout effect, and the shift outlasts the sale (Table 3). The authors put the cost at about CHF 1,000 per additional aligned referendum vote; no federal referendum outcome changed.

The discussion question worth having ready: why did Basel readers leave a repositioned paper while US viewers stayed with a station whose local news shrank? A famous party leader as buyer versus an invisible corporate deal; ideology versus local-ness; a newspaper with nearby substitutes versus TV habit. The answer decides whether audiences can be relied on to limit owners, or whether that job falls to competition rules. Both papers also revisit Gentzkow and Shapiro (session 4), who found that owners explain little of slant on average.

What you should be able to do afterwards

  • State Media Consolidation's three findings in one line each: content depends on the owner, ads rise, viewers stay
  • Explain what market-by-month fixed effects rule out, and what they cannot (rivals reacting to the new owner)
  • Say why a null on political knowledge is weak evidence in this setting
  • State the Basel chain with one number per link: content, circulation, vote share
  • Give your own answer to why the two audiences reacted so differently
