What the reading does

Three probability lectures described data-generating processes. This one supplies the tool for fitting them: a model is fitted by optimising a function, and optimising is calculus.

Derivatives as rates of change. The derivative is the local slope: how much the output moves per unit of input. Section 3.3 is the toolkit (power, product, quotient rules), and 3.4's two excerpts are the part a policy student already knows under another name: marginal cost is C′(x), marginal revenue is R′(x), and the derivative at x approximates the cost or revenue of the next unit.

The chain rule. For f(g(x)), the derivative is f′(g(x))·g′(x): rates multiply through a chain. Every model later in the course is a chain of this kind (parameters, then a prediction, then a loss), and differentiating the loss with respect to the parameters is the chain rule applied repeatedly.

The exponential and the logarithm. eˣ is its own derivative: growth proportional to size. The derivative of ln g(x) is g′(x)/g(x), a relative change, which is why log points read as percentages. And logs turn products into sums before you differentiate, which is exactly the move maximum likelihood makes in session 6.

Maxima and minima. Critical points are where the derivative is zero or undefined. Fermat's theorem says a smooth local extremum has a zero derivative, but not the reverse (x³ at zero). The Extreme Value Theorem guarantees an absolute maximum and minimum for a continuous function on a closed interval, and they are found by comparing the critical points and the endpoints.

The shape of a graph. The first derivative test reads the sign change of f′ around a critical point; the second derivative test reads the curvature, and says nothing when f″ is zero there. Concavity is what makes a likelihood have a single peak, and the curvature at the peak later becomes a measure of precision.

Power series and Taylor series. A smooth function is, locally, a line or a parabola: the Taylor polynomial matches its value and first few derivatives at a point, and the remainder term says how fast the approximation degrades with distance. Two series to know by heart: 1/(1 − x) as the geometric series, and eˣ as the sum of xⁿ/n!. The second is why the Poisson probabilities, which open lab 4, add up to one.

What you should be able to do afterwards

  • Differentiate products, quotients, compositions, eˣ and ln x by hand without hesitating
  • Read a derivative as a marginal effect and use it to approximate the next unit
  • Find critical points, including where the derivative is undefined
  • Find absolute extrema on a closed interval, endpoints included
  • Classify a critical point with either derivative test, and say when the second fails
  • Find where a function is concave up or down, and its inflection points
  • Write a second-order Taylor polynomial, and the series for eˣ and 1/(1 − x)
  • Explain why taking logs before differentiating a product is legitimate
