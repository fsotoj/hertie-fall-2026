Introduction to the course

Wednesday 2 September 2026, 12:00–14:00, room 2.32. Arianna Ornaghi. No required reading. This is the session where the debate papers and roles are handed out, so 45% of the grade acquires its dates today.

The reading that is not required

The syllabus puts "Required" in quotation marks for this session and recommends three surveys instead. Each one covers a different third of the course, and skimming any of them makes the twelve-session arc legible.

Aridor, Jiménez-Durán, Levy and Song (2024), "The Economics of Social Media", Journal of Economic Literature 62(4), pp. 1422–1474. A guide to the social media literature organised by the life cycle of content: production (incentives, moderation of harmful content), distribution (network structure, algorithms, targeted ads) and consumption (effects on individuals and on society, substitution across platforms). It maps onto sessions 8 to 12 almost directly.

Strömberg (2015), "Media and Politics", Annual Review of Economics 7(1), pp. 173–205. The traditional-media half of the course, with the focus on welfare effects, and a discussion of what the behavioural theories of media effects developed outside economics imply. Sessions 2 to 6 live here.

Zhuravskaya, Petrova and Enikolopov (2020), "Political Effects of the Internet and Social Media", Annual Review of Economics 12, pp. 415–438. What distinguishes online media from offline: low barriers to entry, user-generated content. Reviews effects on voting, protest, attitudes to government, polarisation, xenophobia and politicians' behaviour, plus false news and the tools autocracies use for censorship and surveillance.

What this session decides

The syllabus says the specific papers and roles "will be provided at the beginning of the course". That is this session.

  • Which two debates you are in, and in which role. Everyone does both, once as critic (25%) and once as defender (20%). The critic delivers a ten-minute presentation of the paper and raises a structured critique; the defender answers it. Both roles submit a short essay in advance, and both deadlines hang off the debate date, which is a Wednesday.
  • Whether the work is individual or in small groups. The syllabus leaves it open and ties it to class size.
  • The research design brief. In small teams, an empirical research design for a media-related question: the question motivated clearly, the design set out with its strengths and, the syllabus emphasises, its weaknesses. Due 11 December 2026, with instructions released the week of 2 November.

That date sits on the last day of exam week (7–11 December), which is also the last day at the Friedrichstrasse campus. It is a Moodle submission, not an onsite one, but the week is already full.

How the course runs

Seminar format almost throughout: a short presentation of a research paper by a student or by Ornaghi, then discussion aimed at the methodological and policy issues rather than at the topic in the abstract. Participation is 10%, and the syllabus states plainly that cold calling is used, framed as support for engagement rather than a penalty, with uncertainty an acceptable answer.

The prerequisites are the working assumption of every session: microeconomics at the level of Economics I to follow the framework, statistics and regression at the level of Statistics I to read the tables, and quasi-experimental methods for causal inference, for which Ornaghi strongly recommends Statistics II.

Two absences out of twelve, with no obligation to notify in advance, and the same "if no course assignments are affected" condition the other syllabi carry. A debate session is a course assignment.

Looking ahead

Session 2, Wednesday 9 September, is accountability, and it is the first with required papers: Banerjee, Enevoldsen, Pande and Walton (2024) on public information as an incentive for politicians, experimental evidence from Delhi elections, and Snyder and Strömberg (2010) on press coverage and political accountability in the United States.

The pairing is deliberate: a field experiment next to an observational design built on newspaper market geography, both after the same object. Reading them side by side is the course's method in one week.
