Social media and well-being

Wednesday 28 October, 12:00–14:00, room 2.32. Two papers on how to measure the welfare effects of social media.

Required reading

  • Allcott, Braghieri, Eichmeyer and Gentzkow (2020), The Welfare Effects of Social Media, AER 110(3), 629–676.
  • Bursztyn, Handel, Jiménez-Durán and Roth (2025), When Product Markets Become Collective Traps: The Case of Social Media, AER 115(12), 4105–4136.

What connects them

Allcott et al.'s abstract describes a randomized four-week Facebook deactivation experiment before the 2018 US midterms. It reports higher subjective well-being, lower news knowledge and political polarization, and lower subsequent use.

Bursztyn et al.'s abstract describes incentivized experiments with college students on TikTok and Instagram. Accounting for externalities on nonusers can turn positive conventional welfare estimates negative. Someone can prefer using a platform while others use it and still prefer a world without it.

These summaries orient the reading; they are not full-paper reviews. Check the samples, estimates, compliance and limitations in the assigned papers.
