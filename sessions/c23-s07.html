What the deck is likely to cover

From the reading and from how lectures 2 and 6 set it up. A guess until Tuesday.

Bayes' rule for a parameter. Lecture 2 used Bayes' rule on events (the disease test, Monty Hall). The same rule with an unknown parameter θ in place of the event: the posterior is proportional to the prior times the likelihood. The data enter only through the likelihood, the same object as last week's L(θ).

The black box again. Lecture 6 found that the likelihood of 14 wins in 41 has the shape of a Beta(15, 28). Read this week, that curve is the posterior under a flat prior. With a Beta(α, β) prior, y successes in n trials give a Beta(α + y, β + n − y) posterior: the same family again, which is what conjugate means.

The posterior as a compromise. Its mean, (α + y)/(α + β + n), always sits between the prior mean and the maximum likelihood estimate, and the prior matters less and less as n grows. Yesterday's posterior is today's prior: updating one observation at a time gives the same answer as all at once.

Summaries. Posterior mean, median and mode; a 95% credible interval, which means what people wish a confidence interval meant: a 95% probability that the parameter is inside. Possibly also Normal data with known variance (precisions add) and the Gamma prior for Poisson counts.

Worked examples from the book

  • A carrier of hemophilia? Prior one half; two unaffected sons are four times likelier if she is not; posterior 0.20. A third unaffected son, starting from 0.20: 0.111.
  • 'radom' typed. Word frequencies as the prior, typing errors as the likelihood: 0.325 random, 0.002 radon, 0.673 radom. The likelihood is not a distribution over the three words.
  • Girls born with placenta previa: 437 of 980. Flat prior, posterior Beta(438, 544): mean 0.446, 95% interval [0.415, 0.477], which leaves out the population's 0.485.
  • Asthma deaths: 3 in 200,000, Gamma(3, 5) prior, posterior Gamma(6, 7): mean 0.86 per 100,000, against a crude rate of 1.5. Strong shrinkage from one year of data.

What you should be able to do afterwards

  • Write posterior ∝ prior × likelihood and normalise it, on a grid or by recognising the density
  • Update a Beta prior with Binomial data, and a Gamma prior with Poisson counts, by hand
  • Say why the posterior mean sits between the prior mean and the maximum likelihood estimate
  • Read a credible interval and say how it differs from a confidence interval
  • Show that sequential updating equals one batch update
  • Combine a Normal prior with Normal data by adding precisions
